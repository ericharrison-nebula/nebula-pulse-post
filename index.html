Part I

The Containerization Landscape: A Map of the Territory

The modern containerization ecosystem is a patchwork of tools born from different eras and different problems. Before we can understand where things are headed, we have to understand where we've been — and what each platform was trying to solve.

Foundation
Docker

The tool that started it all. Docker made containerization accessible to every developer with a single CLI. It standardized the image format and gave teams a reproducible way to ship software. But Docker is a runtime, not an orchestrator — it doesn't answer the question of how you run containers at scale.

Enterprise Default
Kubernetes

Kubernetes became the de facto standard for container orchestration. It's powerful, extensible, and battle-hardened. It's also notoriously complex — YAML-heavy, resource-hungry, and requiring dedicated platform engineers to operate well. For many teams, Kubernetes is the right answer. For many more, it's vast overkill.

The Simpler Path
Docker Swarm

Docker Swarm gave teams native clustering within the Docker ecosystem. Simpler than Kubernetes, familiar to anyone who already knew Docker, and easy to spin up. It fell out of favor partly due to Docker Inc.'s turbulent history and partly because Kubernetes absorbed the industry's mindshare — not necessarily because Swarm was wrong.

Flexible Workloads
HashiCorp Nomad

Nomad took a different angle — a single binary orchestrator that handles containers, VMs, and raw binaries. Its simplicity and flexibility are genuine strengths, and it integrates deeply with Vault and Consul. But its ecosystem is smaller, adoption is niche, and building on top of it requires significant self-investment.

Managed Cloud
AWS ECS / Fargate

ECS and Fargate remove the node management headache by outsourcing it to AWS. No cluster to maintain, serverless container execution on Fargate. The tradeoff? Deep vendor lock-in, pricing that scales steeply with usage, and limited flexibility outside the AWS ecosystem.

class="platform-card">
Fully Managed
Google Cloud Run

Cloud Run is the logical endpoint of "just run my container." Deploy an image, get a URL. It abstracts away everything. But it's purely serverless — there's no cluster, no node awareness, no persistent workload control. It's a deployment surface, not an orchestration platform.

Each of these tools carved out a legitimate niche. And yet, between the raw simplicity of Docker Compose, the enterprise complexity of Kubernetes, and the managed-but-locked cloud offerings, a real gap has persisted for years — particularly for teams that want real orchestration without the operational weight.

Part II

The Gap Nobody Fixed

Here's the uncomfortable truth the tooling ecosystem doesn't like to talk about: the vast majority of containerized workloads in production don't need Kubernetes. They need something between docker-compose up and a full RBAC-enabled, multi-tenant, node-autoscaling Kubernetes cluster — something capable, modern, and manageable by a team that isn't a dedicated platform engineering org.

"

Engineering teams shouldn't need a Kubernetes certification and three dedicated engineers just to scale a web service. The complexity tax is real — and it's being paid in engineering hours that could go toward building product.

Docker Swarm was — and still is — genuinely good at this middle ground. It's operationally lightweight, Docker-native, and dramatically simpler to reason about than Kubernetes. The problem isn't Swarm itself. The problem is that nobody has built a great management layer on top of it.

Teams running Swarm in production often find themselves stitching together custom scripts for autoscaling, hacking together authentication flows for their dashboards, and manually wiring CI/CD pipelines to deployments. The infrastructure works — but the experience is rough. Observability is limited. GitOps is an afterthought. Scaling requires hands on keyboards.

This is the gap. A gap between the powerful-but-exhausting world of Kubernetes and the simple-but-limited world of unmanaged Docker Swarm. Teams operating here have been underserved — until now.

These aren't edge-case problems — they're the daily friction that erodes team velocity and forces premature migration to heavier platforms. Nebula Pulse attacks all three simultaneously.

Part V

The containerization industry has spent a decade telling teams that complexity is the price of capability. That if you want autoscaling, GitOps, and real security, you have to pay for it in YAML files, control plane overhead, and a team of platform engineers. That has never been entirely true — it was just the only deal available.

Nebula Pulse is a bet that a massive segment of the market was simply underserved. The teams running Swarm — the lean engineering orgs, the startups, the mid-market companies who aren't Google but do need their containers to behave — deserve tooling built specifically for them. Not a stripped-down version of Kubernetes. Not a set of DIY scripts. A platform that starts with Docker Swarm's elegant simplicity and adds exactly the capabilities that make it production-grade.

The era of "you either go full Kubernetes or you figure it out yourself" is ending. Nebula Pulse is what comes next — and it's exactly what Docker Swarm was always meant to have sitting on top of it.
